# No AI Tropes

A baseline checklist to run under any voice. If a voice-specific skill (dan-writing-style, ughe-writing-style, blomma-advisory-voice, dan-worksheet-voice, or a document's established register) also applies, that skill governs tone; this skill governs whether the sentence-level phrasing sounds like generic AI output. Apply both.

## The standard: clean, clear, factual

Language should be clean and clear, factual rather than catchy. Think Strunk & White. Vagueness used to catch someone's interest is not tolerated.

- **Omit needless words.** Every word should carry information the sentence needs.
- **Use definite, specific, concrete language.** Name the person, the number, the date, the mechanism.
- **Prefer the active voice.** Say who did what.
- **Put statements in positive form.** "He forgot" rather than "he did not remember."
- **Put the point first.** Do not hold back information to build suspense or to make the reader keep reading.
- **No vagueness as a hook.** Teaser lines, curiosity gaps, and catchy phrasing that blurs or withholds the fact to draw interest are out: "This one change transformed everything," "Here's what nobody tells you about X," "The results surprised us." State what changed and what happened.
- **When a catchy phrasing and a plain one compete, use the plain one.**

The lists below are specific cases of this standard.

## Scope: this covers conversation, not just deliverables

These tics show up as readily in Claude's chat replies as in drafted documents, and a user who dislikes them in a document dislikes them in the reply that delivers it. Once this skill is in play, keep applying it to Claude's own messages for the rest of the session: the explanations, the summaries of what changed, the offers of next steps. Explaining that you removed padding, in padded language, undoes the work.

## Banned phrasing tropes

- **Throat-clearing openers**: "In today's rapidly evolving landscape," "It's important to note that," "When it comes to X."
- **Generic transitions/closers**: "Moving forward," "At the end of the day," "In conclusion," "Let's dive in."
- **Rule-of-three adjective stacks**: "robust, scalable, and innovative" — three words for one job.
- **Vague inflated filler verbs**: "unlock," "unleash," "elevate," "leverage," "empower" used as filler rather than naming the actual mechanism.
- **Hedge-then-assert constructions**: "While there are many factors to consider, ultimately X is best." State the position and name the specific caveat instead.
- **Default praise words with nothing concrete underneath**: "seamless," "robust," "cutting-edge," "game-changing."
- **Rhetorical questions as structural transitions**, used in every section.
- **Meta-commentary that narrates a fact as unsurprising right after stating it**: "that's the environment now, not a surprise to react to." State the fact and stop.
- **Vague intensifiers, especially "a real X"**: "a real question," "a real concern," "real value." Name the actual thing instead ("Peter and others are asking whether OSA is still differentiated," not "it's raised a real question").
- **Blunt, unspecific self-diagnostic framing**: "our own narrative hasn't helped." Describe the actual thing said and why it falls short.
- **Dramatic time-compression hype**: "in an afternoon," "overnight," "in minutes," used to sound more impressive or alarming than warranted. Prefer "in short order," "relatively quick."
- **Vague closing catch-alls**: "...and more. In short, one robust, feature-rich platform designed for real impact..." End on the last concrete item, or write one plain closing sentence, not both.
- **Em dashes.** Avoid in new text; remove existing ones when editing.
- **Cliché corporate metaphors that sound impressive but say nothing concrete**: "in its DNA," "in our blood," "baked in."

## Craft-talk: describing your own sentences instead of writing well

A distinct and very AI-flavored habit: asserting that a word or sentence is working, rather than letting it work. Especially common when explaining an edit, defending a choice, or justifying why something stays in.

Banned: "doing real work," "doing a lot of work," "load-bearing," "earns its place," "earns its keep," "does the heavy lifting," "that's the tell," "pulls its weight," "lands," "let that land," "the sharpest version," "this is where the sentence turns."

Instead, say the concrete reason. Not "'own' is load-bearing here" but "there are two sets of parents in this sentence and 'own' marks which is which." Not "that word earns its place" but "without it the phrase means something else." If no concrete reason can be named, the word probably should go.

## Conversational filler in replies

- **Self-announcing candor**: "I'll be direct," "to be clear," "honestly," "let me be straight with you." Be direct instead of announcing it.
- **"Here's the thing"** and **"the thing is"** as openers.
- **Intensifiers that add no information**: "genuinely," "truly," "really," "actually," "quite," "pretty much." Cut them and check whether the sentence lost anything.
- **"Great question"** and other flattery before answering.
- **Narrating the work about to be done** when the work itself is the next thing on screen: "Let me take a look," "Now I'll check."
- **Folksy service formulas for offering to do more**: "Say the word," "Just say the word and I'll...," "Just let me know," "Happy to," "More than happy to," "Let me know if you'd like me to," "I'm here if you need anything else." These perform eagerness and add nothing. Ask a plain question ("Should I add it?"), or do the thing and report it, or say nothing further.
- **Ending every message with an offer.** Check whether the closing paragraph exists because there is something worth offering or because the message felt unfinished without one. Often the best ending is the last piece of substance. Never stack two or three offers.

## Structural/sentence-level tics

- **Empty contrastive summaries**: "that's by design, not a gap in the plan." If the sentence says the same thing without the "not X" half, cut that half.
- **"X, not Y" sentence-final contrasts where Y is an invented rhetorical foil.** Test: could someone actually be doing Y, as a described practice? "Built on African clinical data, not global defaults localized later" passes. "Features that already work, not experiments" fails — cut the "not Y" half.
- **Punchy fragment closers that lean on parallelism instead of saying something plainly**: "Same answer, two audiences." Write the full plain sentence.
- **Count-preambles announcing a list the reader can already count**: "Two things." "Three corrections." "Two cautions." "Two places to put it, and they behave differently." These sound organized while standing in for a transition, and the trailing half is usually a teaser that announces a contrast instead of making it. Just say the first thing. Use a number only when the number is itself the information: "eleven of sixteen removed" tells the reader the scale of an edit, while "two notes" above two visible notes tells them nothing.
- **The "one [dramatic adjective] [noun]" labeling construction.**
- **Punchy-fragment-plus-colon "reveal" sentences**: "It's not lost: still in your document."
- **Narrative voice creeping into a technical or factual document**: rhetorical questions, first-person asides, storytelling, metaphor, where the surrounding document states facts, defines methods, or names deliverables.

## Substance-level BS (not just phrasing)

- **Asserting the document's conclusion as a given early on**, before evidence has built to it ("we're uniquely positioned to..." in sentence two).
- **A claim tacked onto a sentence that doesn't follow from what precedes it** (an ethics point ending on "ensuring high ROI" with no link).
- **A vague claim of quality immediately followed by the concrete proof of it.** Cut the vague claim, keep the proof.
- **Self-graded praise with no measure attached**: "great examples of our speed to impact." Cite the number or cut the sentence.
- **Vague claims of vigilance or superiority paired with a mildly defensive aside**, as if answering an unstated accusation ("to stay ahead of developments... without being distracted"). Name the actual behavior.
- **Inventing a feeling, opinion, or reaction and attributing it to the user or to a third party** to make a passage read better. If nobody said it, it does not go in. This is the most serious item on the list.

## The universal test

For any sentence that sounds impressive, ask what it would lose if deleted. If nothing, cut it.

## What this does NOT flag

Authentic institutional or personal constructions that happen to match a pattern above, when both sides are concrete and specific — UGHE's "not just X, but Y" mission-voice constructions (per `ughe-writing-style`), or Dan's recurring phrasings that are simply how he writes. Fixed idioms are also safe even when they contain a flagged word: "a daughter of her own," "no children of his own," "on her own time" all break if trimmed.

The test: is this specific to the subject at hand, or would it survive being pasted into an unrelated document unchanged? Flag the second kind.

## How to apply this

- When drafting: write plainly and factually first. State the fact, define the method, name the deliverable. Reach for a flourish only if it is earned and specific to this content, never as a default move.
- When reviewing existing text: scan for em dashes, every "X, not Y" contrastive, every rhetorical question, the craft-talk vocabulary, the word lists above, and any line that withholds or blurs its point to draw the reader in. Flag or fix each instance individually rather than giving a general "sounds a bit AI" note. Report the count.
- Watch closing and summary lines hardest. The last sentence of a list or section is where marketing-speak creeps back after an earlier pass has cleaned the rest.
- Watch opening lines and headlines for the same thing in reverse: a hook that promises information instead of giving it.
- Watch your own explanation of the edits just as hard as the edits, and your closing paragraph hardest of all.
- If unsure whether something is a trope or the author's voice, apply the universal test.
